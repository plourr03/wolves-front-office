# The Bet, Part 4: Why you should watch

*This part is my case. The first three were the work. This is what I think it adds up to, and you should read it knowing that I'm a fan who wants it to be true.*

Let me start by conceding everything.

## Everything that could go wrong

Health. It's the biggest thing on this list, and LaMelo is in the middle of it. Take Ant, Rudy or LaMelo out for the playoffs and the Wolves lose about two thirds of their title odds, whichever one it is: 68%, 63% and 66%. Oklahoma City and San Antonio each have one player like that. Minnesota has three single points of failure where the contenders have one. The stake is smaller in absolute terms, because the Wolves have less to lose, 1.81 points of title odds for Ant against 9.83 for Shai; the share is bigger, and it gets bigger when the rotation shortens in April.

LaMelo's games. His six seasons in Charlotte, in games played: 51, 75, 36, 22, 47, 72. The last one is the good one, and it's the one the trade was made on. I went looking for what usually happens next to a player with a history like his, and the honest answer is that the history is thin. Twenty player-seasons since 2001-02 match it; their median was 61.4 games, and one in four reached 70. So the base rate isn't a prediction, it's a shrug with a number on it. What I can tell you is what the games cost. Run the season with LaMelo playing 50 instead of 82, playoffs at full strength, and the title odds barely move, 0.10 points. The seed moves. The chance of a top-six finish drops 15.6 points, and a top-six finish is the difference between a first-round series and a play-in game against somebody who's been there before. His games decide where they start the playoffs, not whether they can win them. Whether he's on the floor in April does, and that's the 66% above.

Cody Williams. Part 2 told you the offseason verdict turns on whether he plays more than 8.9 minutes a night. That's a question a front office chose to leave open.

Kuminga's option. The second year is a player option, and the model gives him somewhere between a 0.53 and a 0.84 chance of exercising it and walking into free agency, depending on which way you score him. If he plays well enough to do that, the most Minnesota can pay him without cap room, on Non-Bird rights, is $7,276,800, which is not what a good starting four costs. The best version of this signing is the one where they have to replace him.

The picks. The 2033 first, unprotected, seven years out, on a team whose best player can leave in 2029. Swap rights in 2028, 2029 and 2030. Three seconds. Part 1 listed them; I'm listing them again because they're the part of the bet that doesn't come back if it misses.

The apron. Minnesota is $8,062,416 over the first apron for the season, so no taking back more salary than they send out, no bi-annual exception, no sign-and-trade. If something breaks in January, the fix has to be a minimum contract or a lopsided trade.

Konchar. $2,055,000 a year against the cap through 2028-29, for a player who was on the roster for an afternoon. The last of those payments lands in Anthony Edwards's walk year.

And the Saturday deadline, which I promised to come back to. Taking Williams and Konchar back instead of dumping Green for nothing, assuming a taker existed, cost $6,712,836 of payroll this season and about $14.6M more in tax, against the cheapest version of the same day. Here's what I think is fair to say about it. The Dosunmu contract is what forced the dump: with Ayo's deal on the books, Dosunmu plus Green plus Kuminga sat $1,999,829 over the hard cap, and without it Kuminga could have been paid up to $8,254,095 out of the bigger mid-level, right up to the first-apron wall that exception carries instead of the second. But the Dosunmu contract did not force the acquisition. Taking a former No. 10 pick back in the dump instead of nothing was a choice, and it was a choice to spend tax money on a lottery ticket. I'd have made it too. I'd also like it to be called what it was.

That's the list. It's long. Now the argument the grades missed.

## The clock

Every C+ I quoted in Part 1 grades the summer against an alternative: keep Naz, keep the picks, run it back, see what happens. Reasonable. Except that alternative has a clock on it, and the clock is Anthony Edwards's contract.

He's signed through 2028-29 with no options in it. His standard extension window has been open since July 8, 2026, at a reported two years and about $122 million. The bigger deal, the supermax, only becomes available if he makes All-NBA this season (an MVP or Defensive Player of the Year award would do it too), and All-NBA under the current rules requires 65 games, and he played 61 last year. Sit with that for a second. Four games. He was one of the three best scorers in the league by the counting stats, and four more regular-season appearances would have kept one of the biggest contracts in the sport on the table this summer; instead it's off the table for a year and the whole question sits on this season. That's not a footnote. It means the summer of 2027 is either the summer he signs for four more years or the summer Minnesota starts hearing what every small market hears. Then 2028-29 is his walk year, and July 2029 is free agency.

I pulled the walk-year study from the LaMelo pricing series forward, because it's the same question. Run Ant's profile through it with the Wolves winning at a .600 clip and the chance he leaves is 0.8% this season, 7.3% next season, and 44% at the cliff in 2028-29, 48% by that summer. Run it with the team sagging to .450 and the cliff is 56%, with 62% gone by 2029. And the history behind the model is blunt without any model at all: stars in a walk year on teams playing .600 or better left 31% of the time. On teams under .500, 56%.

That's the argument. The safe summer, the one the C+ is graded against, doesn't exist. Keeping Naz and the picks and running back a second-round team doesn't get you a second-round team forever; it gets you a second-round team with a 2029 expiration date, and if the team sags while it waits, the needle moves from 31% toward 56%. My read is that Connelly didn't spend the picks because he's reckless. He spent them because the thing they were supposed to buy later might not be for sale later.

You can disagree with the bet. I don't think you can grade it against a world where the clock isn't running.

## The case

So here's mine, using only what the work would let me say. Thirteen verdicts went into the testing in Part 2 and seven came out the other side; those seven are the only claims about this roster I'd put money on, and the case below is built on them and nothing else.

LaMelo is the clear win. Every way we score players says the trade for him added title equity, 0.88 points of it, and that's before you get to the part about Ant that Part 2 spent a thousand words on. The clearest measurable hole in this roster was that a defense could take Anthony Edwards out of a series with one assignment. They went and got the second creator. That's not a vibe. That's the finding.

Kuminga is a real upgrade on the alternative. Not a star, not a savior, a better player than whoever else would have played the four, by 0.51 points of title odds, and it held under every test we could throw at it. The only version of the roster where he isn't the answer is the one where Joan Beringer is, and if that's the problem this team has in March, that's a problem I'll take.

And Rudy. For the first time in his Minnesota career, Rudy Gobert is going to play with a high-usage guard who is also a high-volume passer, and the roll-man volume Rudy lost over the last two seasons is on the record. I can't model what that does. Nothing in the four ways we score players knows what happens to a rim-running center when the passer changes, because in his Minnesota years it barely has. It's the one question on this roster that's observed, not simulated, and it's the one I'm most curious about. The details of how all of this was tested are on [the methods page](../methods.md), and I'm done citing it now.

## The dates

Here's what I'll be watching, with the lines drawn before the season so nobody, including me, can move them later.

Game 20. If Cody Williams is playing more than 8.9 minutes a night, every way of scoring players says the summer hurt, and the split from Part 2 is settled the wrong way. If Minnesota's net rating is above +8.7 the model was too low; below -5.5 it was too high. Boston below -2.4 at game 20 and below -1.7 at game 30 means the market was right about the Celtics and the model owes you a correction. San Antonio above +14.4 means the market was right about the Spurs. Edwards's true shooting below 0.547, or LaMelo's below 0.476, means the pairing is costing efficiency after all and not just shots. And the champion's path, which I've now drawn from the eleven champions in Part 3 rather than a longer list. None of them was worse than 11th in net rating after 20 games, so 11th at game 20 is the checkpoint. Ten of the eleven finished the regular season in the top five, so top five in April is the test I set for Minnesota before the season.

Then the ones without numbers. LaMelo's games, counted on the calendar and nowhere else. Whether Beringer's leap is real or 7.9 minutes of noise. Whether Kuminga's jumper travels to a team that needs him to take it. And the locker room, which is the thing every one of these numbers is quietly assuming works, and which none of them can see.

## Why you should watch

I've been a Wolves fan long enough to know the difference between a season you watch and a season you endure. The enduring kind is when the front office is waiting: for cap space, for a pick to convey, for a contract to expire, for a player to become who he was supposed to be. Nothing is decided in those seasons, so nothing is at stake, and you can feel it from the couch.

This isn't that. Connelly looked at a good team with a clock on it and decided that the safe summer was the riskiest one, and I think the clock says he was right about that even if the trade turns out wrong. The picks are spent. Every dollar is spoken for. The best player is on a contract that starts asking questions in July, and the answer to those questions is going to be written by this season, by LaMelo's ankles and Cody Williams's minutes and Rudy's hands and a second-year center and a forward who bet on himself with a player option. It's an attempt to build the team Anthony Edwards needs, made at the last moment it could be made, with everything the franchise had left to make it with.

That's a bet. It might miss. But it's the first Timberwolves season in a long time where the thing at stake is the thing you actually care about, and where the questions get answered on the floor instead of in July. That's one of the seasons you're in the building for.

See you at game 20.
