# The Bet, Part 2: What we found

The first time I ran the season, the model told me the summer made us worse.

I want to be honest about what I did next, because it's the whole reason this piece exists. I ran it again. Same answer. Then I sat there for a while with the feeling every Wolves fan knows, the one where you've already decided how you feel about something and the evidence shows up late and disagrees with you, and I had to choose between the evidence and the feeling. I chose to go find out why. This part is what I found, and about a third of it is the model being wrong in ways I can show you, which is not a thing you get from the grades.

Here's the question I actually get asked, usually somewhere between the second beer and the check. Did the summer make them better or worse?

I've spent a month trying to answer it properly, and the honest answer is a story. It depends on a twenty-one-year-old from Utah, and it depends on how you count an Achilles tendon.

A word on how we got there, because it matters for everything below. We didn't grade the moves. We priced them. Every roster in the league was rebuilt from the box scores, every player scored four different ways, and the season was run 200,000 times per way, with and without each thing Minnesota did this summer. A finding only counts if it comes out the same under all four ways of scoring players, under both assumptions about how players age, under both rules for handing out minutes, and by more than the simulation's own noise. Most of what we tried didn't survive that. What's below did, or I'll tell you it didn't.

## The kid from Utah

Start with the ledger as a basketball problem instead of a cap problem. Randle out, Reid out, Conley and Anderson gone, Donte DiVincenzo on crutches. LaMelo in, Kuminga in, Cody Williams in. Run the roster that finished last season against the roster that will start this one, and the four different ways we score players mostly agree the summer cost Minnesota something. Mostly, and only if you take every player at last season's level. Correct the whole league for how players age, which helps a young team, and even that much agreement goes away. So the word doing the work in "worse" turns out to be a name. Cody Williams.

I'd never thought about Cody Williams for more than a minute before August 29. He was the No. 10 pick two summers ago, a long wing with a jumper the scouts kept promising, and he came to Minnesota on the last Saturday of August as the piece Utah put into the Green dump, the throw-in that made the arithmetic work. The numbers from his two Utah seasons are rough. The model scores him at -4.39 points per hundred possessions, and only one player in the league's projected rotations is rated lower. The front office says it sees untapped potential, which is what you say about a former lottery pick who arrived in a salary dump; the honest version is that they took him because he's young and long and the alternative was nothing.

And then the model gives Cody Williams 16.1 minutes a night. That's a real role for a player the numbers didn't like in Utah, and it isn't a judgment about him. It's what happens when the wing rotation has a hole in it and somebody has to fill it. Below 12.6 a night, the four ways of scoring players stop agreeing that the summer hurt. Not because Williams is secretly good, but because the minutes he doesn't play go to players the model trusts more, and once that happens the ledger is close enough to even that the four ways split on it. So the sentence "the offseason made Minnesota worse" is true only while a kid from Utah is playing a real role. Which he might. Finch has to play somebody, and after the starters and Kuminga the model's bench is Bones Hyland, Cody Williams, Jaylen Clark and Beringer, four guys competing for the minutes Donte would have had. The model's guess is that Williams wins a chunk of them. A coach's guess in October might be different, and a coach's guess in January will be different again, because it will have forty games of evidence the model doesn't. That's not a flaw in the method. It's what the method is for: telling you which fork in the road matters before you get to it.

## Weather

Now the Achilles, which I keep wanting to skip past and can't.

Game 4 against Denver, in a series Minnesota won. Donte goes down, and you know from the way he goes down. He'll miss most or all of this season, and the front office didn't bring in an established wing to cover for him. You can count that as a decision, since they could have, or you can count it as weather, since nobody plans for a torn Achilles in April. The model says it costs 0.39 points of title odds on its own. Count it against the summer and the summer looks worse. Count it as weather and the summer roughly breaks even.

Here's the thing I didn't expect when I pulled it apart. Those aren't two separate problems. A healthy Donte is exactly the player who takes Cody Williams's minutes. The injury and the kid's role are the same wound viewed from two sides, and every argument about whether the summer was good or bad is secretly an argument about who plays the wing minutes in November.

So my honest answer to the bar question is: ask me at game twenty. I know that's not the answer anybody wants at the bar. It's the true one.

## What I'm sure about

That's the part that's uncertain. There's a lot that isn't, and I want to be as plain about the sure things as I was about the shaky ones, because the sure things are why I'm not worried the way the grades are.

Kuminga first. The question isn't whether Jonathan Kuminga is a great player. The question is whether he's better than whoever else would have played the four, and the answer is yes, and it held under every test we could throw at it. Better by 0.44 points of title odds, or 0.56 once you correct for how players age. Different ways of scoring players, both aging assumptions, both rules for handing out minutes, all of it. You can read how that testing works on [the methods page](../methods.md); I'll spare you the grid here. The one thing that flips it is Beringer. If Joan Beringer takes those minutes instead, the model would rather have Beringer, and it would rather have him by a lot. It says that on the strength of 7.9 minutes a game of rookie evidence. Which is either the most interesting fact on this roster or a small sample doing what small samples do. Probably both. Beringer is very young. We'll see.

Then the trade itself, which I can finally say something clean about. LaMelo Ball in is the one move on the ledger that is clearly positive: 0.79 points of title odds, 0.91 aged, and every way of scoring players agrees. Naz Reid out is clearly negative: 0.33 points the other way, 0.40 aged, and again every way of scoring players agrees. Randle out doesn't get a sign, because the four ways of scoring Julius Randle can't agree on whether he was helping. The Dosunmu re-sign doesn't get a sign for the same reason, the four ways split on Ayo, and what that contract really cost is a cap story for Part 4. So if you want the trade in one sentence: they got the point guard, and Naz, not the picks, was the real basketball price. I've made my peace with the picks. I have not made my peace with Naz, and I'm not going to pretend the numbers made me.

## The May I keep coming back to

The third thing I'm sure about is Anthony Edwards, and this one starts on a couch in May.

If you watched the San Antonio series you already know the shape of it. The Spurs put one defender on Ant, ran him off the three-point line, and made him make plays out of the space between the free-throw line and the rim. He had good nights. He also had nights where the whole offense was him trying to beat a scheme by himself, because there was nobody else on the floor the defense had to account for as a creator, and you could see it on his face by the fourth quarter. I remember thinking it looked like a problem a roster causes, not a problem a player causes. I didn't have a way to check that in May. I do now.

We looked at what happens to the top scorers in the league when a good defender is assigned to them. For most stars, the answer is a little. For Ant, the answer is more than for almost any of them, and over three seasons more than for any of them. Of the 150 highest-volume scorers last season, Edwards sits at the 1st percentile: almost nobody in the league loses more to his primary defender than he does. Widen it to three seasons and 245 scorers and he is the lowest of all of them, the single most defender-suppressed scorer in the sample. We counted 21 different primary defenders assigned to him last season, so this isn't one bad matchup; it's what happens whoever the assignment is. That's not a stat about Ant's talent. It's a stat about what a defense can do to a player, and the simplest reading of it is that there was nobody else on the floor who could create a shot.

Which is what the rest of the numbers say too. Last season 61% of Ant's makes were unassisted. LaMelo, in Charlotte, was at 55%. The league median is 35%. Minnesota just put two of the most self-created scorers in basketball in the same backcourt, and the obvious worry is that they'll get in each other's way. I had that worry. The base rate doesn't. When a high-usage player gains a new high-usage teammate, the player who was already there loses usage and doesn't lose efficiency: at the star level, 1.5 points of usage, and true shooting actually went up 0.8 points, across nine cases. Fewer shots, not worse ones. And a starting five with Kuminga in it has a combined usage at the 96th percentile of 7,380 starting fives we could measure, which is the polite way of saying somebody is going to give up shots. The base rate says that's fine. Somebody always does.

That's why Ball is here. Not because the front office fell in love with a highlight reel. Because the single most measurable weakness on the roster was that a defense could take Anthony Edwards out of a series by assigning one good player to him, and the fix for that is a second creator. Whether LaMelo is the right second creator is a fair question. That the job needed filling isn't. If you take nothing else from this part, take that: the trade was an answer to the thing I watched happen in May.

## The arguments we have in September

Now the part that surprised me, and honestly humbled me a little, because I went in wanting to prove the podcasts right.

Most of the things fans argue about in September, the things I argue about, can't be measured. We tried. I want to be careful about what that sentence means. It doesn't mean size and pace and matchups don't exist, or that coaches are wasting their time. It means that once you know how good two teams are, knowing their style doesn't help you predict who wins, at least not with the tools I have and the seasons we've got. That's a statement about prediction, not about basketball. But it's a statement the odds are quietly making too, and it turns out to be right.

We built a style model, the one everybody wants: pace, three-point rate, rim rate, offensive rebounding, turnovers forced, size, the stuff you'd put on a whiteboard. Adding it to the predictions made them worse. Not much worse. Worse.

We took eight regular-season traits that are supposed to translate to the playoffs, the ones you hear every May, and tested them on 195 playoff series going back to 2014. Zero of the eight predicted who won the series once you knew how good the teams were. Not the three-point rate. Not the size. Not the pace. Not the thing your favorite podcast is sure about.

"Defense travels" is not supported, and the estimate points the other way: a team leaning on its defense rather than its offense loses 0.85 points a game in the playoffs for every standard deviation of that lean, which is short of the bar we set for calling it real, so we don't. But it's not pointing where the cliché says.

Versatility, the idea that some teams match up well against more of the field, turns out to be net rating restated. The better team is the better team against everybody.

And the individual matchups, the "who guards Ant" stuff, are the same story in miniature. Of 79 real primary matchups across the West's contenders, two beat the norm by enough to notice, which is about what you'd get by chance.

So when someone tells you the Wolves will be fine because they're big, or doomed because they're small, ask them which of the eight it is. I've started doing this. It has not made me more popular.

## The night the model was wrong in public

I said a third of this piece is the model being wrong. Here's the biggest one, and I'm telling it because a model that's never been wrong in public is a model nobody has checked.

The model had the Hornets at 5.86% to win the title. Charlotte. A team the market prices at 0.81%. I stared at that number for a long time, and I'll admit my first instinct was to look for a reason it might be right. Then, while checking the lineup data for a different reason entirely, I found it. The pipeline that builds lineup numbers from play-by-play was crediting some baskets to the wrong team, 3.36% of all points on the 578 team-games I checked, and the same mistake lived one level down in the possession data every player rating is built on. So I fixed it at the source, refit every player in the league on corrected points, and reran the whole thing. Charlotte came down to 3.44%, still above the market, still a disagreement, but an honest one now. Minnesota's number barely moved, which is the part that let me sleep.

So here's the honest scoreboard, after the fix. The model has Minnesota at 1.68% to win the title on the primary basis, 2.39% once you correct for age, and on the primary basis every one of the four ways we score players ranks Minnesota below where the market does (correct for age and one of the four climbs above it). The market says 3.16% and sixth in the league. The model says 13th to 16th. That's the disagreement, and I'm not going to pretend it away by picking the aged number.

The bigger disagreement isn't about Minnesota at all. It's Boston. The model has the Celtics at 18.33% to win the title against a market price of 5.47%, and it's the same story however you score the players. Somebody is wrong, and we'll know by December: if Boston's net rating is below -1.7 through game 30, the market was right and the model owes you an explanation.

If you want to know whether to trust the model, don't watch Minnesota. Watch Boston.

## Health, and the path

Two short beats before I hand off, because they're the two things people will actually ask me about in January.

Health. If you take any one of Minnesota's three most important players out for the playoffs, the Wolves lose 54% of their title odds, 43% once you correct for age. That sounds fragile and it is. But the loss is spread across three players rather than one: removing Ant, LaMelo or Rudy costs 0.87 points of title odds on average, and no one of them is meaningfully worse to lose than the others. And when you tighten to a playoff rotation, Minnesota loses the least of the three West contenders we ran. This is not a one-man team, whatever it used to be. After the last three Aprils, I'll take that sentence and frame it.

The path. The most likely seed is 7th, at 31%, which in the current format means the most likely version of April is a play-in game, one night, everything on it. Top six, 42%. If they make the field, San Antonio or Oklahoma City is the first-round opponent 53% of the time, which is the whole problem in one sentence. They reach the second round 28% of the time, and from there, having escaped the first round, they win it all 6.1% of the time. The mountain is the first round, and it has Wembanyama or Shai standing on it.

So I got curious. If the odds can't see what wins, what did the last eleven champions actually have? That's Part 3.
