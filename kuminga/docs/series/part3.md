# The Bet, Part 3: How champions actually happen

I went looking for a pattern. Eleven champions, 2015-16 through last June, every one of them rebuilt from the box scores and the transaction logs: who played the playoff minutes, how each of those eight players got there, how old they were, how many had been there the year before, where the team sat in net rating in October and after the All-Star break and in June, how many games the eight missed, how much the minutes tightened when it mattered. I cross-checked every champion against the record before I used a row, and the definitions are on [the methods page](../methods.md). And for each of them, the price: where the preseason title odds had them in October, all thirty teams, every season, from the same archive.

Here they are, in order, and then what they have in common.

## 2015-16 Cleveland

In October the market had them as the favorite, at 22.95%. The Cavaliers went 57-25, the No. 1 seed in the East, 4th in the league in net rating at +6.3. After the break they were 2nd, at the same +6.3, and in the playoffs they were +9.5, a full 3.2 better than the regular season. The top eight by playoff minutes: 2 drafted (Kyrie, Tristan Thompson), 3 traded for (Love, JR Smith, Shumpert), 3 signed (LeBron, Richard Jefferson, Dellavedova). Average age 28.0, the oldest 35.6. Seven of the eight had played for the franchise the season before, and returning players took 86% of the playoff minutes. The eight missed 88 regular-season games between them and 2 of 21 in the playoffs. The top five's share of the minutes went from 59% to 71%. No in-season move touched the eight. Then they came back from three games to one down against a Warriors team that had won more regular-season games than anyone ever had.

## 2016-17 Golden State

In October the market had them as the favorite, at 50.93%. The Warriors were the most complete team on the list: 67-15, the No. 1 seed, 1st in net rating at +11.4, still 1st after the break at +9.2, and +12.8 in the playoffs, 1.5 better than the regular season. The top eight: 3 drafted (Curry, Klay, Draymond), 1 traded for (Iguodala), 4 signed (Durant, West, Livingston, Ian Clark). Age 29.7, the oldest 36.4. Six of eight returning, 67% of the playoff minutes. They missed 64 regular-season games and 7 of 17 playoff games. Top five 60% to 68%. No in-season move. They lost one playoff game.

## 2017-18 Golden State

Same core, a stranger year. In October the market had them as the favorite at 57.65%, the highest price of any champion on this list. 58-24, the No. 2 seed, 3rd in net rating at +5.9. After the break they fell to 16th at +0.9, down 5.0, the kind of second half that gets a lesser team written off. In the playoffs they were +10.8, 4.8 better than their regular season. The eight: 4 drafted, 1 traded for, 3 signed. Age 29.5. Seven of eight returning, 89% of the minutes. They missed 113 regular-season games and 15 of 21 playoff games, the playoff absences nearly all Curry and Iguodala. Top five 53% to 67%. No in-season move. They swept Cleveland.

## 2018-19 Toronto

The trade-built champion. In October the market had them fifth, at 4.54%. 58-24, the No. 2 seed, 3rd in net rating at +5.8, 3rd after the break at +7.2, up 1.5. In the playoffs +5.6, 0.2 worse than the regular season, the only champion on this list whose playoff level was essentially its regular-season level. The eight: 1 drafted (Siakam), 6 traded for (Kawhi, Lowry, Gasol, Danny Green, Ibaka, Powell), 1 signed (VanVleet). Age 28.9. Five of eight returning, 56% of the minutes, the lowest continuity on the list until Milwaukee and Denver. They missed 93 regular-season games, 22 of them Kawhi's, and 1 of 24 in the playoffs. Top five 56% to 72%. One in-season move touched the eight: Marc Gasol, traded for on February 7. They beat Golden State in six.

## 2019-20 Los Angeles

The bubble champion. In October the market had them second, at 15.41%, just behind the Clippers. 52-19 in the shortened season, the No. 1 seed, 5th in net rating at +5.6. After the break, 10th at +1.0, down 4.6, with the season stopping in the middle of it. In the playoffs +6.9, 1.4 better. The eight: 0 drafted, 2 traded for (Anthony Davis, Kuzma), 6 signed (LeBron, Caldwell-Pope, Danny Green, Caruso, Rondo, Markieff Morris), the most free-agent-built champion here. Age 29.5, oldest 35.1. Five of eight returning, 58% of the minutes. They missed 61 regular-season games and 5 of 21 playoff games. Top five 55% to 63%. One in-season move: Morris, signed February 23. They beat Miami in six.

## 2020-21 Milwaukee

In October the market had them second, at 12.75%, behind the Lakers. 46-26, the No. 3 seed, 4th in net rating at +5.8, 5th after the break at +5.1, down 0.7. In the playoffs +5.3, 0.5 worse than the regular season. The eight: 1 drafted (Giannis), 3 traded for (Middleton, Holiday, Tucker), 4 signed (Lopez, Connaughton, Portis, Forbes). Age 29.6. Four of eight returning, 55% of the minutes. They missed 54 regular-season games, the fewest on the list, and 8 of 23 playoff games, Giannis among them. Top five 57% to 72%. One in-season move: P.J. Tucker, traded for March 19. They lost the first two to Phoenix and won four straight.

## 2021-22 Golden State

The one the numbers liked least in the spring. In October the market had them tied for third, at 8.19%. 53-29, the No. 3 seed, 4th in net rating at +5.5, and after the break 18th at +2.0, down 3.5, the worst second half of any champion here. In the playoffs +4.9, 0.6 worse than the regular season. The eight: 5 drafted (Curry, Klay, Draymond, Poole, Looney), 1 traded for (Wiggins), 2 signed (Porter, Gary Payton II). Age 28.9. Six of eight returning, 70% of the minutes. They missed 152 regular-season games between them, 50 of those Klay's and 37 Draymond's, and 13 of 22 playoff games. Top five 51% to 69%. Payton was signed on opening night, so he counts as the in-season move. They beat Boston in six.

## 2022-23 Denver

The champion the regular-season numbers underrated most. In October the market had them ninth, at 4.01%, the lowest price of any champion on this list. 53-29 and the No. 1 seed in the West, but only 6th in net rating at +3.3, the lowest of the eleven, and after the break 16th at +0.4, down 3.0. Then +8.0 in the playoffs, 4.7 better than the regular season, with a top five that took 76% of the minutes, up from 57%, as tight as any rotation on the list. The eight: 4 drafted (Murray, Jokić, Porter, Braun), 2 traded for (Gordon, Caldwell-Pope), 2 signed (Bruce Brown, Jeff Green). Age 27.6, oldest 36.4. Four of eight returning, 52% of the minutes, the lowest continuity here, partly because Murray had missed the whole previous season and counted as new, and Brown, Caldwell-Pope and Braun were all new too. They missed 105 regular-season games and 1 of 20 playoff games. No in-season move. They beat Miami in five.

## 2023-24 Boston

In October the market had them as a co-favorite with Denver, at 14.69%. 64-18, the No. 1 seed, 1st in net rating at +11.7, and 1st after the break at +14.6, up 3.0, the best second half on the list. In the playoffs +8.6, 3.0 worse than the regular season, which is what happens when you're that good and the opponents get better. The eight: 3 drafted (Tatum, Brown, Pritchard), 4 traded for (Holiday, White, Horford, Porziņģis), 1 signed (Hauser). Age 29.3, the oldest 37.7. Six of eight returning, 76% of the minutes. They missed 87 regular-season games and 12 of 19 playoff games, every one of the playoff absences Porziņģis. Top five 58% to 76%. No in-season move. They beat Dallas in five.

## 2024-25 Oklahoma City

The youngest champion here by a distance. In October the market had them second, at 10.80%, behind Boston. 68-14, the No. 1 seed, 1st in net rating at +12.7, 1st after the break at +12.4. In the playoffs +8.6, 4.1 worse than the regular season, and they still won. The eight: 3 drafted (Jalen Williams, Holmgren, Aaron Wiggins), 3 traded for (Shai, Caruso, Cason Wallace), 2 signed (Dort, Hartenstein). Age 25.5, the oldest 30.9. Six of eight returning, 78% of the minutes. They missed 154 regular-season games, the most on the list, 50 of them Holmgren's, and 1 of 23 playoff games. Top five 54% to 65%. No in-season move. They beat Indiana in seven.

## 2025-26 New York

And then the Knicks, the one that was on television three months ago, and the one I keep staring at.

In October the market had them fourth in the league at 8.27% to win it. The win total was 53.5. They won 53. Our model, run back on last season's rosters, had them at 4.63%, below the market, which is worth remembering the next time I tell you the model has Minnesota below the market too. They were a steady team: +6.33 a game across the season, +6.16 before the break and +6.67 after it, 5th in net rating, the No. 3 seed in the East. Nothing about the regular season said "champion." It said "good."

Then the playoffs happened. They went 16-3 at +14.89 a game. Of the sixteen teams in the field, they were the only one whose margin improved from the regular season. The top five had missed 46 games between them from October to April; in the playoffs they missed 2. The top five's share of the minutes went from 0.579 to 0.673, the same tightening every champion on this list did, but on a team that had been merely good at 0.579. The top eight: 1 drafted (Mitchell Robinson), 5 traded for (Hart, Bridges, Anunoby, Towns, McBride), 2 signed (Brunson, Shamet). Age 28.8, the oldest 30.9. All eight had been there the year before, and returning players took 91% of the playoff minutes, the highest continuity on the list. No in-season move. They beat San Antonio in five for the title.

If you want the shortest version of what a champion looks like in June, it's that paragraph: the same eight guys, healthy, playing more of the minutes, better than they were in February.

I keep coming back to the 4.63%, because it's the number that should make me humble. Our model, given last October's rosters, had the Knicks below the market and nowhere near the favorite, and it was wrong, and it was wrong in a way I recognize. It saw a team that was 5th in net rating and priced it like one, which is what a model should do. What it couldn't see was that the version of the Knicks that mattered, the one with the top five healthy and playing 0.673 of the minutes, had barely existed during the regular season, because those five had missed 46 games between them. The market couldn't see it either, at 8.27%. Nobody could. That's not an argument against models or markets. It's an argument for watching the spring instead of the winter.

## The pattern

Now put them side by side.

Six of the eleven were No. 1 seeds. Ten of the eleven were top five in regular-season net rating, and the one that wasn't, Denver, was 6th. Five were top three. The second half of the season told you much less: the post-All-Star rank of a champion was as bad as 18th (the 2022 Warriors) and 16th twice (the 2018 Warriors, the 2023 Nuggets). Age ran from 25.5 to 29.7, averaging 28.7. On average 5.8 of the eight had been there the year before. On average the eight missed 101 regular-season games and 6.2 playoff games, which is the whole list in two numbers: champions get hurt in the winter and are healthy in the spring. And every one of them tightened: the top five's share of the minutes went from an average of 56% in the regular season to 70% in the playoffs. An in-season move touched the eight for 4 of the 11, and not one of the four changed who the team's best player was; it was Gasol, Morris, Tucker, Payton, a starter or a rotation piece each time.

There are really two kinds of champion on this list. There are the ones the regular season told the truth about: the 2017 Warriors, the 2024 Celtics, the 2025 Thunder, 1st in net rating in October and 1st in April, teams that were worse in the playoffs than in the regular season because the playoffs were harder and they were still the best team in them. And there are the ones the regular season lied about, or at least mumbled: the 2018 Warriors at 16th after the break, the 2022 Warriors at 18th, the 2023 Nuggets at 6th for the year and 16th after the break, teams that looked like they'd lost a step and then played their best basketball of the season in May and June. What the second group had in common wasn't style. It was that the eight guys were healthy in April, and the coach could play five of them 70% of the minutes.

What about the price? I have it for all eleven now. The favorite won four of the eleven, and one of those four, Boston in 2024, shared the top of the board with Denver. Eight of the eleven came from the market's top three and ten from its top five; the exception was Denver in 2023, ninth at 4.01%. The prices ran from that 4.01% up to 57.65% for the 2018 Warriors, with the middle of the list at 12.75%. So the market's top five is where champions come from, and the price inside that group tells you less than you'd think: a team priced at 4.01% and a team priced at 57.65% both won. The feature comparison is a narrower thing, and I've kept it to the three most recent seasons, where I have every measurable feature for every team. When I took the fourteen teams the market had in its top five over those three seasons that didn't win, and asked which of twenty measurable features separated the champions from them, three did: offensive rank, defensive rank, and continuity. None of the style features did. Not pace, not size, not three-point rate, not any of the things we argue about. Being good on both ends, and having been together, is what separated the champions from the other favorites.

## What the list says about a 3.16% team

So turn back to Minnesota, priced at 3.16% and sixth in the league. That's a lower price than any of the eleven started with, and Denver's 4.01% in 2023 is the closest. By rank it reads better: sixth is inside the top nine every champion came from. The list doesn't say a team like that can't win; it says what the season has to produce first. It has to become a top-five net-rating team by April, because ten of eleven were. It has to get healthy in the spring after being hurt in the winter, because all of them did. It has to be able to tighten to five players who can carry 70% of the minutes without the floor falling out, which is a very specific question about a roster whose depth chart thins out fast after the top three. And it helps, though it doesn't decide anything, to have been together, which this group has not.

The market is pricing a team that might do that. The model is pricing a team that probably won't. Neither of them can see the thing the list keeps pointing at, which is that the difference between the Knicks in February and the Knicks in June wasn't the roster. It was the same eight guys, healthy, playing more.

Whether Minnesota has eight guys like that is the question I'll try to answer in the last part. That one isn't the work. It's my case.
