# The Wolves' Offseason Is an Apron Decision Before It Is a Trade Decision

_Piece 1 of the Wolves to a T offseason series. The three lines that decide the summer, the rule
that forces the first domino, a thinner pick chest than advertised, and what the roster actually
needs. Every number below traces to a public source or to our own cap model, and the methodology
behind all of it lives [here](#methodology). Draft date June 11, 2026. Figures confirm-on-publish._

---

The most important number of the Timberwolves' offseason is not 28, the pick they hold in this
month's draft. It is not 49, the wins, or 31.4, the usage rate Anthony Edwards dragged around all
season like a piano on his back. It is $209,100,000.

That is the NBA's first apron line for 2026-27. Our cap model has the Wolves' books at $194.0M,
about $15 million underneath it. Almost everything Minnesota wants to do this summer, every tool,
every exception, every two-for-one consolidation, lives or dies inside that $15 million. Most of
this offseason was decided before a single trade call gets made, by a line the Wolves have not
crossed yet and cannot afford to cross carelessly.

The season that set this up needs only a paragraph. The Wolves won 49 games, took the 6 seed, and
beat Denver in six in the first round, outscoring the Nuggets by 27 across the series. Then San
Antonio outscored them by 97 points over six games. Not a bad bounce or a hot shooting series.
Ninety-seven points. Finch said the team ran out of bullets, and the box scores agree: Edwards
carried a 31.4% usage rate through a season in which he missed a career-high 21 games, and Donte
DiVincenzo, the roster's connective shooter and second ball-mover, tore his Achilles and is
expected to miss most or all of next season. That is the team the front office is now reshaping.

So before anyone argues names, here is the board the argument happens on.

## Three lines, one margin

The CBA draws three lines above the salary cap, and each one takes something away.

| line | 2026-27 figure | Wolves' distance | what crossing it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Luxury tax | $201.0M | $7.0M under | money (and repeater history later) |
| **First apron** | **$209.1M** | **$15.1M under** | **the toolbox: full midlevel, trade exceptions, sign-and-trades** |
| Second apron | $222.0M | $28.0M under | team-building itself: no aggregating salaries, no cash, a frozen future first |

(Books at $194.0M per our model as of June 9, eight guaranteed contracts plus roster charges.
ESPN's Bobby Marks pegs the first-apron gap near $14M; the difference is roster-charge accounting,
and nothing below changes either way.)

The tax line costs Glen Taylor's successors money. The aprons cost the basketball operation its
hands. And the important mechanical fact is that the aprons do not just punish you for being over
them. They reach down and grab you from above through hard caps. Use the wrong tool while you are
safely under the line and the line becomes a ceiling you are legally barred from crossing all
season. Which tools trigger which ceiling is the whole game:

- **The full midlevel exception ($15.0M):** sign a real free agent with it and you are hard-capped
  at the first apron, $209.1M, all year.
- **The trade exceptions:** the Wolves hold two live ones worth using, $10.8M (from the February
  Conley trade) and $6.6M (from the Dillingham trade), plus a $62K crumb of last summer's
  Walker-Alexander exception that expires in July. Absorb a player into either real one and the
  first-apron hard cap snaps on.
- **A sign-and-trade acquisition:** same trigger, same ceiling.
- **The taxpayer midlevel ($6.1M):** the small version of the MLE hard-caps at the second apron
  instead, $222M. It is the tool you are left with when the first apron is already gone.

Fifteen million under the line, with a toolbox whose every tool pins you to that line the moment
you use it. Hold that thought, because the next fact is the one that spends the margin.

## The Dosunmu rule

At the February deadline the Wolves restructured their guard rotation in one move with Chicago:
Mike Conley and Rob Dillingham out, four second-round picks out, Ayo Dosunmu in, absorbed into the
trade exception left over from the Nickeil Alexander-Walker sign-and-trade. It was a quiet deal at
the time. It is the entire fulcrum of the offseason now.

Dosunmu was good here. In 24 regular-season games as a Wolf he gave them 28.5 minutes a night,
14.4 points, and shot 41.4% from three. In the playoffs he got better: 15.6 points and 4.1 assists
across 10 games, 50% from the field, 42.5% from deep, while spending long defensive possessions on
the other team's best guard. On a roster missing DiVincenzo and now missing Conley, he is the
returning answer at the position, and he is an unrestricted free agent the Wolves paid four
seconds to rent.

*The receipts. Regular season as a Wolf in gray, playoffs in green. The playoff version was the
better one, which is exactly what you want from the player you are about to pay.*

Re-signing him is the easy part, mechanically. Minnesota holds his Bird rights, which let a team
exceed the cap to keep its own free agent, no hard cap attached. The trap is what his new salary
does to the geometry. Pay Dosunmu a market number in the mid-teens and the books climb from
$194.0M to roughly $210M. That is over the first apron. Nothing illegal happens by itself, but
every first-apron tool dies on contact: the $15.0M midlevel becomes unusable, both trade
exceptions become unusable, sign-and-trades are off the menu. The Wolves would enter July with
their best free agent retained and their entire toolbox sealed in glass, left holding the $6.1M
taxpayer midlevel and veteran minimums.

Bobby Marks put a number on the harder version of the same squeeze: for the Wolves to re-sign
Dosunmu and take in a maximum-level salary without blowing through the apron structure, they must
first send out at least **$58.5M**. There are only two contracts on the roster that get you there,
and only together: Rudy Gobert ($36.5M) and Julius Randle ($33.3M), $69.8M combined. McDaniels
($25.8M) and Reid ($23.3M) are the painful alternates the math will keep suggesting and the
timeline keeps vetoing.

Read the rule plainly and it stops being about Dosunmu at all. **The Wolves cannot keep their best
free agent and use their best tools unless a big salary leaves first.** That is the sentence the
whole summer hangs on. Every Gobert column, every Randle rumor, every draft-night consolidation
idea is downstream of it.

## The chest

The trade columns will spend July sketching packages, so it is worth stating exactly what
Minnesota has to put in them. The pick chest, verified against the league ledger:

{{viz:pick-chest}}

*Every first-round slot on the ledger, 2026 through 2033, with its real status. Hover any card for
the condition. The bars underneath are all 30 teams' cleanly tradable future firsts; the green one
is Minnesota, 29th of 30. The second-round shelf is nearly bare too; four seconds just went to
Chicago in the Dosunmu deal.*

Two clean firsts, one late pick, and conditional scraps. ESPN's league-wide asset rankings put the
Wolves near the bottom for tradable draft capital, and our ledger agrees. This is not a chest that
wins a bidding war for a star. It is a chest that funds one mid-sized consolidation, or seasons a
couple of value trades, and the difference between those two uses is most of what this series will
end up arguing about.

## What the roster actually needs

We rebuilt the need analysis from the warehouse rather than from vibes: six dimensions, measured
for the returning roster (DiVincenzo's absence baked in), benchmarked against how contending
playoff offenses and defenses are actually constructed, then recomputed under each exit scenario,
because what the Wolves need depends entirely on who leaves. The full method is on the
[methodology page](#methodology). The shape of the answer:

{{viz:needs-by-exit}}

*Six dimensions against a contender benchmark, recomputed for each exit door. The columns land one
at a time; the red cell is each scenario's largest hole. Hover any cell for the underlying numbers.*

Two things in that grid cut against the loudest versions of the discourse.

First, the model does not ask for more on-ball creation. Edwards covers it; as-is, it is a
surplus. What the roster lacks is everything that should surround a primary like him: shooting
that survives playoff closeouts, a second playmaker so the offense does not flatline when he sits
or gets blitzed, and guards who can hold a point of attack. The hole is not another hero. It is
the connective tissue heroes need.

Second, the needs are conditional, and the conditions are the trade decision. Move Randle and
secondary playmaking becomes the single largest gap on the team. Move Gobert and the defensive
dimensions blow open, point-of-attack and rim protection at once, on a roster whose only internal
rim answer is a 19-year-old. Move both and five of the six dimensions are holes. Subtraction is
how the Wolves unlock their tools, and subtraction is also how they manufacture brand-new needs.
That tension does not resolve in this piece. It is the series.

And if the model's top line sounds abstract, the head coach said it out loud this week. On KFAN,
in a hit picked up by Yahoo and ClutchPoints, Chris Finch said the Wolves "definitely need another
ball handler and playmaker" to take the load off Edwards. The coach watched the same season the
warehouse did: 31.4% usage, 21 games missed, and an offense with one live dribble too few. When
the gut, the coach, and the model independently land on the same column of the table, that column
is probably real.

## How to watch the summer

Here is the frame this series will use from now through July, and you can use it on every rumor
that crosses your feed. When a Wolves move is reported, do not start with who came back. Start
with three questions. Which side of $209.1M did the books land on? Which tools are still alive?
And did the move pay for Dosunmu, or pay with him?

Everything else, whether Gobert's price is real (it is more complicated than you think, and that
is the next piece), whether the star names survive the physical, what a point guard actually costs,
and what we would do with the whole toolbox, builds on this board. The Wolves are $15 million
under the line that decides all of it. Watch the line, not the names.
