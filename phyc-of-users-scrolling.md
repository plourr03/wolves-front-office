# Stopping the Scroll: The Perceptual Science of What Arrests the Eye, and How to Build It Into a Static Data Slide

## TL;DR
- The visual wins the first battle; the words win the war. In a feed, a stimulus-driven (bottom-up) feature like high contrast, a face, or a single dominant element earns the involuntary eye fixation in the first fraction of a second, and only then does a legible word or number convert that fixation into a read and a click. For a text-and-data slide with no face and no motion, your scroll-stopping power is bottlenecked by visual saliency, not copy quality, which is exactly why a well-written but visually even slide feels skippable.
- The biggest, best-evidenced levers for a 1080x1350 still are: one dominant high-contrast focal element (a giant number or three-word phrase), extreme size and typographic contrast, a color or brightness that breaks the blue-and-white feed pattern, a human face or eyes if one can honestly be added, ruthless removal of competing elements, and a curiosity-gap or "you think X, actually Y" line that is instantly legible at thumbnail size. Faces, contrast, size, and the curiosity/information-gap mechanism are the most strongly supported by peer-reviewed science; specific "thumb-stop rate" percentages from agency blogs are folklore.
- Creative quality, not targeting, is the dominant driver of advertising sales effect: per Nielsen Catalina Solutions' "Five Keys to Advertising Effectiveness" (3rd ed., Aug 2017, ~500 CPG campaigns), creative drives 47% of advertising-driven sales contribution (49% with context), the single largest element ahead of reach (22%) and brand (15%), rising to 56% for digital campaigns. So the effort you put into the slide itself is the highest-leverage thing you can do, and the goal is to engineer one honest, high-arousal, identity-relevant curiosity gap and make it visually unmissable.

## Key Findings

1. Attention is split into bottom-up (stimulus-driven, automatic) and top-down (goal-driven) systems. A scrolling fan is mostly running bottom-up, so you must win on raw stimulus features (contrast, size, color, faces, motion) before their goals ever engage.
2. The visual system extracts the "gist" of a scene astonishingly fast, with meaning detectable at 13 milliseconds per image (Potter et al., 2014). You get one near-instant categorization: "data slide / ad / skip" or "wait, what?" That categorization is visual, not verbal.
3. Faces, and especially eyes with direct gaze, are processed by specialized brain machinery (the fusiform face area) and capture attention faster than objects, even before conscious awareness. Gaze cueing means viewers look where a depicted face looks, so a face looking at your headline pulls the eye to the text.
4. Feeds give you a sub-second window. Facebook IQ "Thumbstopper" research found more than 67% of ads were seen and two-thirds cognitively recognized within 0.4 seconds, with 100% of ads cognitively processed by 3 seconds; Facebook reported users spend an average of just 1.7 seconds with any piece of mobile content (versus 2.5 seconds on desktop).
5. Curiosity is driven by an information gap (Loewenstein, 1994): a gap between what you know and want to know creates a feeling of deprivation that compels you to close it. Open loops (the Zeigarnik effect) keep unfinished things active in memory.
6. High-arousal emotion drives sharing and attention. Awe, anger, and anxiety increase virality; low-arousal sadness decreases it (Berger and Milkman, 2012). Identity and self-relevance ("my team") are powerful attention magnets, analogous to hearing your own name in a crowd (the cocktail party effect).
7. Creative is the dominant lever in advertising effectiveness, which validates pouring effort into the slide itself.

## Details

### 1. The perceptual and cognitive science of visual attention

**Bottom-up versus top-down, and saliency.** Vision science distinguishes two control systems for attention. Bottom-up (stimulus-driven, exogenous) attention is captured automatically by the physical properties of what is in front of you. Top-down (goal-driven, endogenous) attention is steered by what you are looking for. The dominant computational account is the saliency-map model of Koch and Ullman (1985), implemented by Itti, Koch and Niebur (1998) in their paper "A Model of Saliency-Based Visual Attention for Rapid Scene Analysis" (IEEE PAMI). In that model, the visual input is decomposed into separate feature maps (color, intensity/luminance, orientation, and in video, motion), each map highlights locations that differ from their surroundings (center-surround contrast), and the maps are combined into a single topographic "saliency map." Attention then visits locations in order of decreasing salience. The practical takeaway: salience is about local contrast and difference, not absolute properties. A bright element is salient only if its neighbors are darker; a red element pops only against non-red. This is why a slide that is uniformly busy or uniformly even has no salient entry point.

**Feature integration theory and pre-attentive processing.** Anne Treisman and Garry Gelade's Feature Integration Theory (1980) showed that a small set of basic visual features (color, orientation, size/intensity, and motion) are registered "early, automatically, and in parallel" across the whole visual field, before and without focused attention. This is the pre-attentive stage. A target that differs from everything around it on a single feature "pops out": detection is near-instant and does not slow down as you add distractors (feature search). But a target defined by a combination of features requires slow, serial, attention-demanding scanning (conjunction search). For a creator this is the single most important lab finding: one feature contrast (one giant number, one block of color, one face) can be processed pre-attentively and stop the eye, but a slide that requires the viewer to combine several elements to "get it" demands focused attention the scroller will not give.

**Speed: the gist of a scene.** Mary Potter's lab established that the visual system extracts conceptual meaning from a scene extraordinarily fast. In "Detecting meaning in RSVP at 13 ms per picture" (Potter, Wyble, Hagmann and McCourt, Attention, Perception, and Psychophysics, 2014), viewers could detect a named target picture (for example "smiling couple") in a stream presented at just 13 milliseconds per image, performance above chance even when the name was given only after the sequence. We make three to four fixations per second, and each glimpse triggers a fresh feedforward analysis. The implication: a scroller categorizes your slide's gist (its "what it is") in well under a tenth of a second, and that snap judgment is made on the coarse visual layout, not on reading the words. If the gist reads as "generic text slide," the verbal content never gets a hearing.

**Foveal versus peripheral vision and how fixations are triggered.** High-acuity vision is restricted to the central ~2 degrees of the visual field (the fovea); the periphery is low-resolution and blurry but is highly sensitive to low spatial frequencies, motion, and coarse contrast. The job of peripheral vision is to select the next saccade target; the job of the fovea is to identify detail. Roughly three times a second a saccade brings a peripherally detected region of interest into the fovea. This is the mechanical basis of scroll-stopping: a post must throw a coarse, low-spatial-frequency signal (a big bold shape, a strong contrast edge, a face-like blob) that the blurry periphery can detect and that triggers a fixation. Fine detail and small text are invisible in the periphery and cannot stop anyone.

**Faces as attention magnets.** Faces are a special category. The fusiform face area (FFA) is a cortical region specialized for face perception (Kanwisher and colleagues), and newborns preferentially orient to face-like patterns. Direct gaze (eye contact) is detected especially fast: faces with direct gaze reach awareness faster than averted-gaze faces under interocular suppression, engaging a network including the FFA, superior temporal sulcus and amygdala. Gaze cueing means observers automatically shift attention in the direction a seen face is looking. The applied evidence is striking: in eye-tracking of baby-in-ad images (James Breeze's widely cited study of 106 viewers), when the baby looked out at the viewer, attention pooled on the baby's face and the ad copy was largely ignored; when the baby was turned to look at the headline, viewers followed the gaze and the text and brand got far more fixation. A peer-reviewed print-ad eye-tracking study found that face presence plus a model gaze directed toward the product (rather than toward the viewer) strongly increased attention to and memory of the product and brand. Lesson: a face stops the eye, and a face looking at your number drags the eye to your number.

**Eye-tracking patterns and banner blindness.** The Nielsen Norman Group's eye-tracking research established the F-shaped reading pattern for text-heavy pages (users scan the top, then a shorter second line, then skim down the left edge) and the related fact that roughly 79 percent of users scan rather than read word-by-word. Critically, banner blindness (first documented by Benway and Lane, 1998, and confirmed repeatedly by NN/g) shows people learn to ignore anything that looks like an ad, even when it is bright, large, and contains useful information. This is a direct warning for an analytics publication: a slide that pattern-matches to "promotional graphic" can be filtered out pre-attentively before it is even fixated. Looking native, editorial, and unlike a banner is a feature, not a compromise.

### 2. Scroll-stopping mechanics specific to feeds

**The sub-second window.** Facebook IQ "Thumbstopper" research found that more than 67 percent of ads were seen and two-thirds cognitively recognized within 0.4 seconds, with 100 percent of ads cognitively processed by 3 seconds, and that mobile feed content can be recalled at a statistically significant rate after only a fraction of a second of looking. Facebook separately reported that users spend an average of just 1.7 seconds with any given piece of mobile content (versus 2.5 seconds on desktop). Taken together these numbers describe a brutal funnel: under half a second to register that something is there, a second or two to decide whether to stop. You are not writing for a reader; you are bidding for a fixation.

**Pattern interruption and the orienting response.** Novel, unexpected, or incongruous stimuli trigger an orienting response (Pavlov's "what is it?" reflex; Sokolov's orienting reflex), an automatic shift of attention toward the new or surprising. The Von Restorff (isolation) effect shows that an item that breaks from its context is both more attention-getting and better remembered. In a feed dominated by a recognizable visual rhythm (selfies, action photos, the platform's blues and whites, predictable team-logo graphics), a post that breaks that rhythm gets the orienting response. The lever is contrast with the feed, not contrast in the abstract.

**The motion advantage, and what static images must do instead.** Abrupt onsets and motion onsets capture attention and the eyes in a stimulus-driven way (Yantis and Jonides; Abrams and Christ, 2003), even when the motion is task-irrelevant and even under isoluminant conditions, an effect likely tied to detecting animacy. This is the deep reason video and animation out-stop static images: they generate transients the periphery is built to detect. A still image cannot produce a transient, so it must win on the other salient features the periphery detects in a single frame: high luminance/color contrast, large size, a face, and a strong single focal point. A static slide is competing with one hand tied behind its back and therefore has to max out every static lever.

**Contrast, figure-ground, negative space, and the single focal point.** Designers form opinions about a visual in as little as 50 milliseconds (Lindgaard and colleagues). Pre-attentive capture is driven by contrast, unusual scale, and isolation. The figure-ground principle (the brain separates a "figure" from its "ground") and a single dominant focal point give the eye one unambiguous entry. The repeated professional rule, well aligned with the saliency science, is "you cannot emphasize everything": if every element is loud, nothing is salient, because salience is relative. Negative space around a single element raises its salience by removing competitors. Warm, saturated colors (reds, oranges) are reliably salient and tend to dominate their surroundings; red captures attention faster than green or blue in eye-tracking. High brightness and saturation are salient largely independent of hue. But note a nuance from advertising color research: warm colors win the first fixation while cooler colors (blue, green) can sustain longer engagement, so the right move is a warm or high-contrast accent to win the stop, not a wholesale color scheme change.

### 3. The psychology of curiosity and the hook (the verbal/copy side)

**The information gap.** George Loewenstein's information-gap theory of curiosity (1994) holds that curiosity arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's knowledge," and the person "is motivated to obtain the missing information to reduce or eliminate the feeling of deprivation." A small "priming dose" of information increases curiosity, and the gap must feel both real and closable. This is the engine of an honest hook: state enough to make the reader realize there is a specific thing they do not know but a moment ago assumed they did.

**Open loops and the Zeigarnik effect.** Bluma Zeigarnik (1927) found that people remember interrupted or unfinished tasks far better than completed ones; unfinished business creates a cognitive tension ("open loop") that keeps the item active in working memory. This is why a cliffhanger, an unresolved comparison, or a "here is the surprising part, click to see why" pulls attention forward. The slide should open a loop the article closes.

**Specificity, surprising numbers, arousal.** Berger and Milkman's "What Makes Online Content Viral?" (Journal of Marketing Research, 2012), using all New York Times articles over three months, found virality is driven partly by physiological arousal: content evoking high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) is more viral, while low-arousal sadness is less viral, and these effects held controlling for how surprising, interesting, or useful the content was. For a Timberwolves publication, the high-arousal lane that fits anti-clickbait standards is awe (a genuinely astonishing stat) and surprise (a counterintuitive finding), not manufactured anger. Concrete, specific numbers outperform vague claims because they are both more credible and create a sharper gap.

**Identity and self-relevance.** The cocktail party effect (Cherry, 1953; Moray, 1959) shows that personally relevant information, above all your own name, breaks through even unattended channels: in Moray's original study, 4 of 12 participants (33 percent) noticed their own name in the unattended channel, with later replications reporting roughly 29 percent (Röer and Cowan, 2021) to 43 percent (Conway et al., 2001). Self-relevant and tribe-relevant cues are prioritized automatically. For a team-specific publication this is a major and under-used lever: the team's name, a player's face, a recognizable jersey color, or "if you watched last night" cues act like the reader's name, flagging the post as "mine" and pulling a fan's attention out of autopilot.

**Honest gap headlines and the clickbait caveat.** Curiosity-gap headlines work, but the evidence is more nuanced than "always tease." Aubin Le Quéré and Matias, "When curiosity gaps backfire" (Scientific Reports, 2024), a confirmatory analysis of 8,977 headline experiments from the Upworthy Research Archive (full archive = 32,487 experiments), found that increasing headline concreteness would significantly reduce click-through for 50.9 percent of headlines but benefit only 8.7 percent, meaning concreteness and curiosity-gap effects are strongly context-dependent and overly vague teasing can backfire. Facebook also adjusted its algorithm specifically to suppress curiosity-gap clickbait. The honest, well-supported patterns are: lead with a counterintuitive finding ("you think X, but actually Y"), include a concrete number, and make the gap specific enough that the reader trusts the payoff exists. The goal is a true gap, not a withheld one.

### 4. The synthesis the user needs: visual versus verbal

The evidence points to a clear sequence and division of labor:

1. Peripheral detection (pre-fixation): the blurry periphery picks up a coarse contrast, color, size, or face-like signal and triggers a saccade. This is purely visual. No words are read.
2. Foveal fixation and gist (first ~13 to 100 ms after the eye lands): the visual system categorizes the scene. Still visual. The verdict is "interesting / mine / skip."
3. First word or number (next few hundred ms): if the gist verdict was "stay," the largest, highest-contrast piece of text is read. This is the handoff from visual to verbal. A single number or three-word phrase, not a sentence.
4. Decision (within ~1.7 s): the curiosity gap, identity relevance, or arousal in that text converts the fixation into a read-more and a click.

So: the visual stops the scroll, the words earn the click. They are not competitors; they are sequential gates, and you fail at the first gate (visual) before the second (verbal) ever matters. This resolves the user's confusion. The reason a well-written, well-designed but visually even slide feels skippable is that good copy is gate 3, and a slide with no dominant focal element, no face, no motion, and feed-typical coloring never clears gate 1. The copy is never the bottleneck because it is never reached.

The corollary for relative importance: invest first in winning the fixation (one dominant visual element), then in a single instantly legible word/number that opens an honest gap. A beautiful slide with a buried hook loses; a visually arresting slide with one sharp curiosity line wins.

**What this means for a text-and-data slide with no face and no motion.** Such a slide has surrendered two of the strongest natural levers (faces and motion). The available, evidence-backed levers are:
- One giant, high-contrast focal element, ideally a single shocking number, sized to be readable in the blurry periphery and at thumbnail scale.
- Extreme typographic contrast (one huge element, everything else markedly smaller) so the pre-attentive system has an unambiguous figure.
- Strong color or brightness contrast against the feed (a warm or saturated accent, or a distinctive brand color that is not the platform's blue/white), exploiting Von Restorff and pattern interruption.
- A face or human element if one can be honestly added: a player's face, especially eyes, and if possible gaze directed at the key number, to borrow the FFA/gaze-cueing advantage and convert the "data graphic" gist into a "person" gist that dodges banner blindness.
- Ruthless removal of competing elements and generous negative space, because salience is relative and clutter destroys the focal point.
- One instantly legible curiosity-gap or "you think X, actually Y" line that opens an honest information gap and, ideally, carries team/identity relevance.

### 5. Concrete patterns, examples, and teardowns

**Creative is the biggest lever (so the slide is worth the effort).** Nielsen Catalina Solutions' "Five Keys to Advertising Effectiveness" (3rd ed., 2017), a meta-analysis of nearly 500 CPG campaigns, found creative is the single largest contributor to advertising-driven sales at 47 percent of sales contribution (49 percent counting context), ahead of reach (22 percent), brand (15 percent), targeting (9 percent), and recency (5 percent); within digital campaigns specifically, creative's share rises to 56 percent. NCSolutions' 2022 refresh held creative at the top (unchanged at 49 percent of contribution, and 46 percent for social campaigns). The practical reading for a solo publisher: you cannot out-target the platform, but you can out-create the feed, and that is where the return is.

**Attention seconds and the memory threshold.** Karen Nelson-Field's attention research (Amplified Intelligence) ties active attention seconds to sales and memory outcomes, with active attention and short-term advertising strength significantly correlated (reported r = .82). Industry reporting from this body of work holds that memory retention begins around 2.5 seconds of active attention, that a large majority of digital ads fall short of that threshold, and (in the 2025 VCCP Media collaboration "Hacking the Attention Economy") that as little as 1.5 seconds of genuine attention can encode memory when distinctive brand assets are present. The practical implication for a static slide is that you are fighting for a very small number of attention seconds, so the slide must deliver its gist and its hook in roughly the first second or it delivers nothing. (Note: much of this is presented via commissioned industry talks rather than peer-reviewed journals; treat the direction as solid and exact thresholds as estimates.)

**The baby-gaze teardown.** The Breeze eye-tracking demonstration (106 viewers) is the cleanest teardown of the visual-to-verbal handoff: identical ad, two versions. Face-out: attention stuck on the face, copy ignored. Face-turned-to-text: gaze cueing pulled fixation onto the headline and brand. Common denominator with the science: a face stops the eye (gate 1), and gaze direction routes the eye to the words (gate 3 handoff). For a Wolves slide, a player photographed looking toward the stat does the same job.

**Brand presence and the first frames (video, but the principle transfers).** Facebook IQ's "Stand Out in Feed: Optimizing Video Creative on Mobile" (Dec 2017) found a 2x lift in ad recall when branding occurs within the first three seconds, and that "Mobile First" creative (brand and message front-loaded, designed for sound-off, strong early visual) outperformed TV-style "Traditional Narratives" on recall, partly because only 38 percent of the traditional group showed the brand in the opening frames. For a static slide, the equivalent is front-loading: the number and the hook must be the dominant elements, not a logo lockup or a slow build.

**Thumb-stop folklore versus science.** Practitioner benchmarks (a "good" thumb-stop rate of 30 percent, "faces add 30 percent," "UGC openers beat brand shots by 20 to 40 percent") circulate widely on agency blogs (Motion, Madgicx, Coinis) but trace to aggregated DTC reporting and rules of thumb, not controlled studies. Treat them as hypotheses to A/B test, not as established facts. The well-supported science is the layer beneath them: faces, contrast, size, motion/transients, the information gap, arousal, and self-relevance.

## Recommendations

Stage 1 (every slide, non-negotiable, best-supported levers):
1. Pick ONE focal element and make it dominant. Usually a single surprising number or a three-word phrase. Size it so it is legible as a blurry thumbnail. Test: squint or shrink the slide to a postage stamp; if you cannot read the one thing, it is too small or there are too many things.
2. Maximize typographic and tonal contrast. One huge element, everything else clearly subordinate. Three levels of hierarchy maximum.
3. Break the feed's pattern. Use a warm or saturated accent or a distinctive brand color against a clean ground, and avoid looking like a generic promotional banner (dodge banner blindness).
4. Open one honest curiosity gap in instantly legible text: a counterintuitive "you think X, actually Y," or a concrete number that demands explanation. The article must pay it off.

Stage 2 (when the content allows, to add the strongest natural levers):
5. Add a human face, ideally a player with visible eyes, and orient the gaze toward the key number to exploit gaze cueing. This converts the gist from "data graphic" to "person" and dodges banner blindness.
6. Lean into team identity: jersey colors, recognizable faces, "if you watched last night" cues, so a scrolling fan's tribe-relevance filter flags it as "mine."
7. Choose the emotion deliberately: awe (a genuinely astonishing stat) or surprise (a counterintuitive finding), the high-arousal lanes compatible with anti-clickbait standards.

Stage 3 (systematize and measure):
8. A/B test one variable at a time (the number's size, a face versus no face, warm versus cool accent, gap headline versus summary headline). Treat agency "benchmarks" as hypotheses.
9. Track the stop, not just the click: on Instagram, watch reach-to-profile-visit and saves/shares as proxies for whether the slide stopped people, and link clicks for whether the hook converted.

Benchmarks that would change the plan: if slides with faces do not beat faceless slides on saves/profile visits after a fair test, deprioritize faces for your audience and double down on the giant-number-plus-gap pattern. If warm accents do not beat your brand palette, keep the palette and compete on size and gap instead. If concrete-number headlines underperform teasier ones in your specific audience, that matches the Upworthy finding that concreteness effects are context-dependent (concreteness would lower click-through for about half of headlines), so let your own data, not generic advice, set the dial.

## Caveats
- Much feed-specific advice is marketing folklore. Specific thumb-stop-rate percentages and "faces add X percent" claims come from agency blogs and aggregated DTC reporting, not controlled experiments; I have flagged these as hypotheses to test. The underlying perceptual science (saliency, feature integration, face perception, gist, curiosity, arousal) is peer-reviewed and robust.
- Several attention statistics originate from platform-interested parties. Facebook IQ's sub-second recall and first-three-seconds recall figures, and Facebook/Nielsen brand-lift case studies, are commissioned by or serve the platform; treat the direction as informative and the magnitudes with caution. Karen Nelson-Field's attention thresholds come largely from commissioned industry research rather than peer-reviewed journals.
- Curiosity gaps can backfire. The evidence shows concreteness and curiosity-gap effects are context-dependent and that vague teasing erodes trust and can be algorithmically suppressed. Honest, concrete gaps are the safer, better-supported play, especially for a credibility-dependent analytics brand.
- Lab-to-feed transfer is imperfect. Findings like the 13-millisecond gist and abrupt-onset capture come from controlled displays; they explain the mechanism convincingly but the exact numbers do not map one-to-one onto an Instagram feed.
- Individual and audience differences matter. Attention capture is modulated by goals, perceptual load, and prior knowledge; a knowledgeable Wolves fan scanning for team content behaves differently from a random scroller, which is why your own A/B data should override generic rules.
